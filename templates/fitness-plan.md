# Fitness plan — <person or purpose>

<One paragraph: what this plan is for and over what period.>

## Goals

<What the training is for. A usable goal names an outcome, a horizon, and how progress will be
known — "run 10km continuously within four months, at conversational pace", not "get fitter".

Where several goals are pursued, say which leads and what the others concede. Goals that compete
for recovery — maximal strength and endurance, rapid fat loss and strength gain — will be traded off
whether or not the trade is stated, and an unstated trade is resolved by whichever is easier to
measure.>

## Baseline

<Current capacity, relevant training history, and constraints.

Training history is not the same as current capacity: someone returning after a year off has the
fitness of a beginner and the technique and confidence of a trained person, which is the combination
that produces injuries.

Constraints include injuries current and past, conditions, medications, time, equipment, and access.>

## Progression

<What increases, by how much, on what trigger, and what happens when the trigger is not met.

The fourth is the one usually omitted and the one that matters most — it is what lets the plan hold
when a week goes badly, which is when a plan is actually needed.

Prefer one variable at a time. Progression does not have to be session-to-session; weekly or
block-level usually fits the adaptation timescale better.>

## Recovery and Rest

<Rest days, lighter periods, planned deloads, and what to do when recovery is inadequate.

Recovery is not simply elapsed time — it is sleep, adequate energy and protein, and time, against
total load including everything outside training.

Signals that recovery is not keeping pace, read together rather than individually: performance
stalling under unchanged load, resting heart rate elevated against this person's own baseline, sleep
worsening despite opportunity, persistent unusual fatigue, accumulating niggles, frequent minor
illness. The response is to reduce load, not to push through.>

## Pain and Injury Response

<Decided in advance, because the decision is made worst in the moment by someone invested in
finishing.

State: what distinguishes training discomfort from injury pain; what to do on the second — stop the
movement, not merely reduce it; what modification is available, since an all-or-nothing rule gets
ignored; and what warrants evaluation rather than self-management.

Training discomfort is diffuse, in the working muscles, eases when effort stops, and leaves movement
normal. Injury pain is sharp or localised, often at a joint, may persist or worsen with use, and
alters how the person moves.>

## Review Cadence

<When this plan will be reviewed and on what evidence.

Fix the interval in advance. Assess trends across sessions, not the last one — a single session
reflects sleep, food, stress, heat, and where in the week it fell at least as much as it reflects
fitness.

Look at several measures: different qualities improve on different timescales, so a plateau in one is
not a plateau overall. Where a plateau is real, establish why before adding load.>
