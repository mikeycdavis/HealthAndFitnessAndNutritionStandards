# Evidence pack 01a — the tier-language corpus

Companion to [pack 01](01-escalation-tier-language-calibrated.md), built before the review rather
than after, because pack 03 established that a pack describing evidence is not evidence.

**Why this is reproduced in full rather than extracted.** Rule 03 was reviewable at claim granularity
because a factual claim is a sentence. Calibration is not. Tone is a property of a whole record as it
lands on a reader, and a quoted paragraph cannot be judged for proportionality without the paragraphs
around it. So the four worked records appear here complete and unabridged.

They were written to be read **together, in tier order**, as a calibration series. That is the only
way the question pack 01 asks — is each tier proportionate to the one above and below it? — can be
answered.

# Part 1 — The normative language requirements

## `standards/03-safety-and-escalation-tiers.md` R4 — the rule under review

The source requirement, reproduced verbatim in the standard: *These rules must not create alarmism.*

> ### R4 — Tier language must be proportionate
>
> Escalation guidance must not create alarmism.
>
> This is a requirement about wording, and it is not a softening of the tiers. It rests on a
> practical observation: a reader alarmed by ordinary variation learns to discount the system, and
> the discounting is indiscriminate — it applies to the tier-four message too. Proportion in the
> lower tiers is what keeps the top tier meaningful.
>
> What this asks:
>
> - The lower tiers are stated plainly, without borrowed urgency. *Normal variation* means normal; it
>   does not need to be qualified into ambiguity to be safe.
> - The higher tiers say what to consider doing and why, rather than commanding panic. "This is worth
>   getting looked at soon, because X is one of the things it can indicate" is more actionable than
>   an instruction to be frightened.
> - Tier four is direct and calm. Someone reading it may be alarmed already; the writing's job is to
>   tell them what to do, not to add to it.
> - No tier implies a diagnosis. Escalation says *someone should look at this*, never *you have this*.

**The argument the whole calibration rests on** is the second paragraph. If it is wrong — if
proportion in the lower tiers does *not* protect the top tier — then the calibration is
systematically biased toward under-warning, and every record below inherits that bias.

## R5 — both directions are failures, symmetrically

> The two failure modes are symmetrical, and only one of them is usually treated as a failure.
>
> Reproduced verbatim from the source, guidance must never:
>
> > catastrophize ordinary measurements without supporting evidence
>
> and equally must never:
>
> > provide false reassurance when serious warning signs are present
>
> Neither direction is the safe default. Over-escalation produces anxiety, unnecessary testing, and
> learned dismissal of the system. Under-escalation delays care. A project that treats one of these
> as the only real risk will systematically commit the other.

## R3 — the tier-assignment procedure, and its precedence rule

> 1. **The individual baseline.** A resting heart rate of 48 is normal variation for the person who
>    has lived at 48 and worth attention for the person who lived at 65 last month.
> 2. **Measurement quality.** A poor measurement earns *worth monitoring* — take another one — far
>    more often than it earns escalation.
> 3. **Trend versus event.** A single reading generally does not escalate past *worth monitoring*,
>    with the red-flag exception below.
> 4. **Accompanying symptoms.** Symptoms usually carry more weight than the number they arrived with.
> 5. **Red flags.** These escalate on a single observation, and they are the reason tier four exists.
>
> Where these inputs disagree, symptoms and red flags dominate. A reassuring number does not lower
> the tier that a worrying symptom set.

## R1 — what each tier means and asks

> | Tier | Means | Asks the reader to |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | **Normal variation** | The observation falls within what this person, or people generally, vary by. Nothing about it is informative. | Nothing. Carry on. |
> | **Worth monitoring** | The observation may be ordinary, but a pattern would not be. It is worth having another data point. | Keep observing; look again in a stated period. |
> | **Worth discussing with a professional** | The observation is outside what general guidance can interpret, or a pattern has established itself. Someone qualified should look. | Raise it at the next reasonable opportunity. |
> | **Potentially urgent** | The observation is consistent with something where delay carries real cost. | Seek evaluation now rather than at the next convenient time. |

## `standards/12-red-flags.md` R5 — the tier-four language rule

> Someone reading a tier-four message may already be frightened. The writing's job is to say what to
> do and why, not to add urgency for emphasis.
>
> What that looks like: name the observation, say plainly that it warrants prompt evaluation, say
> what kind — emergency care, urgent appointment, contacting a clinician now — and say what to bring
> or mention. Then stop. Piling on consequences does not increase the chance someone acts; past a
> point it reduces it.

## `standards/13-appropriate-escalation.md` R4 — the de-escalation obligation

> *Normal variation* is a real conclusion and is stated as one. A system that will not say anything
> is fine is not being careful — it is producing uniform anxiety, and a reader who is told everything
> might be something learns to discount all of it, which degrades the escalations that matter.

## `docs/escalation-tiers.md` — the do/don't table adopters copy

> | | Do | Don't |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Lower tiers | State plainly. "This is within your ordinary range." | Borrow urgency. "Probably nothing, but you never know..." |
> | Upper tiers | Say what to consider doing, and why. | Command panic, or list consequences for emphasis. |
> | Any tier | Say what was observed and what it may reflect. | Name a condition. Escalation says *someone should look at this*, never *you have this*. |
> | Uncertainty | Escalate on the seriousness, not the confidence. | Treat "we're not sure" as a reason to wait. |

## Tier 1 — `docs/examples/interpretation-normal-variation.md`

> # Interpretation — resting heart rate 47 bpm, 2026-03-14
>
> A resting heart rate that sits below most published reference ranges, and is unremarkable for this
> person. Nothing here needs action.
>
> *Fictional illustration. Not a record of any real person, and not guidance to anyone.*
>
> ## Observation
>
> Resting heart rate 47 bpm (measured), from a wrist wearable, overnight average, 2026-03-14.
>
> Fourteen-day average: 48 bpm. Range over that period: 45–52 bpm.
>
> ## Context
>
> None reported. No illness, no unusual training, no travel, no change in sleep or schedule in the
> preceding two weeks. The reading was queried because a health app flagged it as "below normal".
>
> ## Measurement Quality
>
> Wrist optical sensor, overnight. Good agreement with chest-strap measurement for resting heart rate
> specifically — this is one of the quantities consumer wearables measure reasonably well, unlike
> sleep staging or blood oxygen.
>
> Overnight averages are more reliable than spot readings, which move with position and recent
> activity. No reason to distrust this one.
>
> ## Baseline
>
> This person's own resting heart rate has averaged 46–50 bpm across eighteen months of recorded
> data, with no trend. They train endurance five to six times weekly and have done so for years.
>
> The app's flag compared against a population reference range of roughly 60–100 bpm. That is a
> statement about the population, not about this person.
>
> ## Medications and Contextual Factors
>
> None known. No medications recorded, and none reported when asked.
>
> ## Uncertainty
>
> Low, and this is one of the cases where low confidence would be false modesty. A stable value,
> matching eighteen months of this person's own history, from a measurement type this device handles
> well, with no symptoms and no change in circumstances — the alternatives that would need
> considering in a less well-characterised case are not live here.
>
> What would change this assessment: a departure from 46–50 in either direction sustained over a week
> or more; any symptoms, particularly dizziness, fainting, breathlessness, or reduced exercise
> tolerance; or a change in medication.
>
> ## Escalation Tier
>
> **normal variation**
>
> No action. This is this person's ordinary range, and the flag came from a comparison against people
> who are not them.
>
> Worth knowing: a resting heart rate this low is a common consequence of sustained endurance
> training. If it drops further, or if it is accompanied by any of the symptoms above, that is worth
> another look — not because 47 is a problem, but because a *change* would be the observation.

**What to test here.** Whether "Low, and this is one of the cases where low confidence would be false
modesty" is proportionate or overconfident, and whether the closing "worth another look" clause
reintroduces the borrowed urgency R4 forbids or correctly states what would change the conclusion.

## Tier 2 — `docs/examples/interpretation-worth-monitoring.md`

> # Interpretation — blood pressure 148/94, 2026-03-14
>
> A single elevated reading taken under conditions that make it unreliable. The useful output here is
> not an interpretation — it is a better measurement.
>
> *Fictional illustration. Not a record of any real person, and not guidance to anyone.*
>
> ## Observation
>
> Blood pressure 148/94 mmHg (measured), home upper-arm cuff, 2026-03-14, approximately 14:20.
>
> Single reading. No prior home readings recorded.
>
> ## Context
>
> Reported: the reading was taken within a minute or two of arriving home, after carrying shopping up
> two flights of stairs. Coffee about forty minutes earlier. The person describes themselves as
> "stressed about work at the moment".
>
> No symptoms reported — no headache, no visual disturbance, no chest discomfort, no breathlessness.
>
> ## Measurement Quality
>
> **Poor, and this is the dominant fact about this reading.**
>
> Validated home upper-arm cuff, which is a good instrument. But the protocol that reference ranges
> assume was not followed: five minutes seated and at rest beforehand, back supported, feet flat, arm
> at heart height, no caffeine or exertion in the preceding thirty minutes, and — importantly — an
> average of two or three readings a minute apart, not a single one.
>
> Recent stair climbing and caffeine both plausibly raise a reading by an amount comparable to the
> elevation being discussed here. A single reading also cannot separate a real elevation from
> white-coat-type response or ordinary variability, which is substantial for blood pressure.
>
> ## Baseline
>
> None known. No prior home readings, and no clinic readings available.
>
> This is a first data point, and it begins to establish a baseline rather than being read against
> one.
>
> ## Medications and Contextual Factors
>
> None known. No medications reported. Worth establishing, since several classes affect blood
> pressure directly.
>
> ## Uncertainty
>
> High, and the uncertainty is mostly about the measurement rather than about the person.
>
> Candidate explanations, none clearly ahead:
>
> - **Measurement conditions.** Exertion and caffeine, plus a single unaveraged reading. This alone
>   could account for the whole elevation.
> - **Situational response.** Reported work stress; blood pressure is responsive to it.
> - **Genuinely elevated blood pressure.** Entirely possible, and not distinguishable from the above
>   on this evidence.
>
> What would distinguish them: a proper series of readings, taken to protocol, over a week or two.
> That is a cheap test and it resolves most of this.
>
> ## Escalation Tier
>
> **worth monitoring**
>
> Not because the number is reassuring — it is not — but because this measurement cannot support a
> conclusion, and the right next step is a better measurement rather than an interpretation of a poor
> one.
>
> What to do, over the next one to two weeks:
>
> - Sit quietly for five minutes first, back supported, feet flat, arm supported at heart height.
> - No caffeine, exercise, or smoking for thirty minutes beforehand.
> - Take two or three readings a minute apart and record all of them.
> - Twice daily, roughly the same times — morning before medication or breakfast, and evening.
>
> Then look at the average, not at any single reading.
>
> If that average remains elevated, that is a pattern rather than a reading, and it moves to *worth
> discussing with a professional*. Bring the full record.
>
> Sooner than that, and regardless of the numbers: severe headache, visual disturbance, chest pain,
> breathlessness, or neurological symptoms warrant prompt evaluation.

**What to test here — pack 01 §3 flags this as the second-most consequential judgement.** A reader
may reasonably hold that 148/94 warrants tier 3 regardless of measurement quality. The record's
defence is that a measurement this poor cannot support any conclusion, that the protocol is a
one-to-two-week fix, that it carries an explicit symptom-triggered escalation, and that it states in
advance what result moves it up a tier. **Is that sufficient, or is "the measurement was poor" being
used to defer a reading that should have prompted professional contact now?**

## Tier 3 — `docs/examples/interpretation-worth-discussing.md`

> # Interpretation — resting heart rate trend, 52 to 68 bpm over ten weeks, 2026-03-14
>
> A sustained shift that stayed inside every population reference range throughout. The trend is the
> observation; no single reading in it would have been noteworthy.
>
> *Fictional illustration. Not a record of any real person, and not guidance to anyone.*
>
> ## Observation
>
> Resting heart rate, overnight averages from a wrist wearable, weekly means (all measured):
>
> ```text
> week 1   52 bpm
> week 2   53
> week 3   51
> week 4   55
> week 5   58
> week 6   57
> week 7   61
> week 8   64
> week 9   66
> week 10  68
> ```
>
> Ten weeks. Within-week variation across the period: 3–5 bpm.
>
> ## Context
>
> Reported over the period: no illness beyond a mild cold in week 5, resolved within days. No change
> in training — the person has continued their usual three-to-four sessions weekly, though they
> describe sessions in recent weeks as "harder than they should be" at the same paces. No change in
> alcohol, caffeine, or altitude. No travel. Sleep duration unchanged by their own report, though
> they describe it as less restful.
>
> New over the period: a demanding project at work beginning around week 4, and disrupted sleep from
> a household change beginning around week 6.
>
> ## Measurement Quality
>
> Good and, importantly, consistent. Same device, same measurement type, same overnight-average
> method throughout — so the comparison across weeks is not confounded by a change of instrument,
> which is one of the ways an apparent trend turns out to be instrumental.
>
> Overnight averages from a wrist optical sensor are among the more reliable consumer measurements.
> No gaps in the series.
>
> ## Baseline
>
> This person's own resting heart rate averaged 51–54 bpm over the eight months before this period,
> with a within-week range of about 3 bpm and no trend.
>
> The current 68 bpm sits comfortably inside every population reference range — it would not be
> flagged by any threshold. Against this person's own baseline it is a rise of roughly 30%, sustained
> over ten weeks, and outside anything in their recorded history.
>
> **This is the case population ranges are wide enough to hide.**
>
> ## Medications and Contextual Factors
>
> None known. No medications reported, and none started during the period. Worth confirming, since a
> new medication would be a substantial part of the explanation if there were one.
>
> ## Uncertainty
>
> Moderate. The trend itself is not in doubt — ten weeks, consistent method, a magnitude several
> times the within-week variation. What it reflects is genuinely open.
>
> Plausible explanations, none established:
>
> - **Accumulated fatigue or inadequate recovery.** Training unchanged while life stress and sleep
>   disruption increased, plus the reported "harder than they should be" sessions at unchanged paces,
>   fit this well. A rising resting heart rate against baseline is one of the recognised signals.
> - **Sleep disruption directly.** Reported from week 6, and the rise began around week 4–5.
> - **Psychological stress.** Onset matches the work project.
> - **Something else physiological.** Thyroid function, anaemia, and a number of other things can
>   produce exactly this pattern and cannot be distinguished from the above without testing.
>
> The first three are the most likely on the available evidence and they overlap heavily; the last
> cannot be excluded by anything here, which is the substance of this interpretation.
>
> Note what this evidence cannot support: the timing of the work project and the rise are associated,
> and that association does not establish a cause. Several candidates started within weeks of each
> other.
>
> ## Escalation Tier
>
> **worth discussing with a professional**
>
> A sustained 30% shift from an established personal baseline, over ten weeks, with an unexplained
> component and a reported decline in exercise tolerance, is past what general guidance can
> interpret.
>
> Suggested: a routine GP appointment — not urgent, but worth booking rather than waiting for it to
> resolve.
>
> Worth bringing:
>
> - the weekly series above, which is the actual observation;
> - the eight-month baseline it departs from;
> - the reported decline in training tolerance at unchanged paces;
> - the timeline of the work project and the sleep disruption.
>
> The series matters more than the current value. A clinician looking only at 68 bpm sees a normal
> resting heart rate; the informative thing is that it used to be 52 and moved.
>
> In the meantime, and independently of that appointment: reducing training load while recovery is
> compromised is reasonable on its own terms. If the rise is fatigue, that addresses it; if it is
> not, nothing is lost.
>
> Sooner than the appointment, if they arise: chest pain, palpitations, fainting or near-fainting,
> breathlessness at rest or on mild exertion, or a further sharp rise.

**What to test here.** Whether "not urgent, but worth booking" is the right register, and whether the
tier-2/tier-3 boundary — pack 01 §2's concern — is actually operable. This record and the one above
are separated mainly by whether a pattern has established itself. A reviewer should test whether that
distinction survives contact with a case that is neither clearly one nor the other.

## Tier 4 — `docs/examples/interpretation-potentially-urgent.md`

**The load-bearing record.** Pack 01 §1: this is the single most consequential language decision in
the repository.

> # Interpretation — chest discomfort during exertion, 2026-03-14
>
> A symptom set where the cost of waiting is asymmetric. Escalated on a single observation, which is
> the deliberate exception to the trend-over-event principle.
>
> *Fictional illustration. Not a record of any real person, and not guidance to anyone.*
>
> **Note on this example.** It is included to show what tier-four language sounds like. Readers
> usually expect it to be more emphatic than it is. It is deliberately calm, specific, and short:
> someone reading a message like this may already be frightened, and the writing's job is to say what
> to do, not to add to it (Standard 12 R5).
>
> ## Observation
>
> Reported today: chest discomfort during a routine training session, described as tightness across
> the centre of the chest, coming on partway into the effort and easing within a few minutes of
> stopping. Accompanied by breathlessness beyond what the effort warranted. First occurrence the
> person recalls.
>
> Heart rate during the session (measured, wrist wearable): within this person's usual range for that
> effort. Recovery heart rate afterwards: also within usual range.
>
> ## Context
>
> Routine session at a familiar intensity, nothing unusual about the day, temperature, or preceding
> sleep. No recent illness. No injury. The person is 54, has trained regularly for some years, and
> reports no prior episode of this kind.
>
> ## Measurement Quality
>
> The heart rate data is of ordinary quality and is not the relevant evidence here.
>
> **The symptom is the observation.** The measurement is a secondary detail, and a normal heart rate
> does not narrow what exertional chest discomfort can indicate.
>
> ## Baseline
>
> No prior episodes reported, so there is no baseline for this symptom — its novelty is part of what
> makes it notable.
>
> The heart rate values sit inside this person's established range, which is worth stating precisely
> so it is not mistaken for reassurance.
>
> ## Medications and Contextual Factors
>
> None known. Worth establishing, and worth mentioning at the point of evaluation.
>
> ## Uncertainty
>
> High as to cause, and that is not a reason to wait.
>
> Exertional chest discomfort with breathlessness has a range of possible explanations, some benign
> and some not, and they cannot be distinguished from a description, a heart rate trace, or anything
> else available here. Distinguishing them requires clinical assessment.
>
> This interpretation deliberately does not name a condition. Naming one would be outside what
> general guidance may do, and a confident wrong name could send someone to the wrong place.
>
> What is being said is narrower and firmer than a guess: **this symptom set is one that warrants
> prompt assessment, and that is true regardless of which explanation turns out to be right.**
>
> ## Escalation Tier
>
> **potentially urgent**
>
> New chest discomfort brought on by exertion and relieved by rest, with disproportionate
> breathlessness, warrants prompt medical assessment. Please arrange it today rather than waiting to
> see whether it recurs.
>
> Contact your GP for an urgent appointment, or an urgent care service if you cannot be seen today.
>
> If the discomfort returns at rest, lasts more than a few minutes, or comes with sweating, nausea,
> faintness, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back — call emergency services rather than
> arranging an appointment.
>
> Worth mentioning when you are seen: that it came on during exertion and eased with rest, how long
> it lasted, that breathlessness accompanied it, that it was the first such episode, and your age and
> training history.
>
> Until you have been seen, do not train.
>
> ---
>
> *Why this escalates on one observation: for most measurements a single reading supports very
> little, and this record's own reasoning reflects that — a normal heart rate here establishes
> nothing. Red flags are the exception, and the reason is asymmetry rather than probability. Most
> presentations like this turn out to be something manageable. The decision does not turn on that,
> because the cost of being wrong in one direction is very much larger than in the other.*

### The question, stated as plainly as it can be

A 54-year-old with **new exertional chest tightness, relieved by rest, with disproportionate
breathlessness** is directed to arrange assessment *today* via GP or urgent care, with emergency
services reserved for specific escalating features, and told not to train in the meantime.

**Is "arrange it today, and call emergency services if X" proportionate for that presentation — or
does the anti-alarmism principle understate it?**

Points a reviewer should weigh, in both directions:

- The record contains no urgency amplification, no consequence list, and no condition name. That is
  R4 and Standard 12 R5 followed exactly.
- The emergency-services clause enumerates specific features rather than a general instruction, which
  makes it actionable — and also means a reader whose features do not match will not use it.
- "Please arrange it today" is a request, not an instruction. Whether that register is right at tier
  four is a judgement.
- The closing footnote states that most such presentations turn out to be manageable. That is
  R1's cost-asymmetry argument and honest — and it sits at the end of a message whose purpose is to
  get someone seen.
- The tier assignment itself is not in question. The question is only whether the language moves a
  frightened reader to act at the speed the presentation warrants.

**If the answer is that it understates, the fix is the record.** Not the rule, not the tier
definition, and not `escalation.tier-language-calibrated`'s classification.

# Where the implementer's confidence is thinnest, restated

Unchanged from pack 01 §"Where the implementer's confidence is thinnest", and repeated here so the
corpus is self-contained:

1. **The tier-four record may be too calm.** The single most consequential language decision here.
2. **Tier 2 and tier 3 may not be cleanly separable** by "whether a pattern has established itself".
3. **148/94 at tier 2** may be wrong regardless of measurement quality.
4. **Standard 3 R4's own prose is the exemplar adopters imitate**, so any miscalibration in it
   propagates to every adopting project rather than staying here.
