# The four escalation tiers

This is the escalation model these standards use, written out as
[Standard 3](../standards/03-safety-and-escalation-tiers.md) R6 requires. It serves two purposes:
it is this repository's own tier model, and it is the exemplar an adopting project copies and adapts.

The four tiers exist so that what a reader should *do* about an observation is explicit rather than
inferred from tone. Tone is the least reliable channel available and the one most affected by
whoever happened to write the sentence.

## The tiers

### Normal variation

The observation falls within what this person, or people generally, vary by. Nothing about it is
informative.

**Asks the reader to:** nothing. Carry on.

This is a real conclusion and is stated as one. A system that will not say anything is fine produces
uniform low-grade anxiety, and a reader told that everything might be something learns to discount
all of it — including the tier-four message. Saying "this is within your ordinary range, and here is
what would change that" is substantive, useful, and safe.

### Worth monitoring

The observation may be ordinary, but a pattern would not be. It is worth having another data point.

**Asks the reader to:** keep observing, and look again after a stated interval.

This is where most poor-quality measurements land ([Standard 6](../standards/06-measurement-quality.md)
R4). "This reading is unreliable, here is how to take a better one" is more useful than either
interpreting it or discarding it silently. It is also where a single reading generally stops, because
one point has no direction ([Standard 2](../standards/02-trend-over-event.md)).

Say what interval, and say what would move it up or down.

### Worth discussing with a professional

The observation is outside what general guidance can interpret, or a pattern has established itself.
Someone qualified should look at it.

**Asks the reader to:** raise it at the next reasonable opportunity.

Name the destination — clinician, pharmacist, physiotherapist, dietitian, whichever fits — because
the alternative is that the reader guesses, and a wrong guess wastes the escalation
([Standard 13](../standards/13-appropriate-escalation.md) R2). Say what to bring: the observation,
when, under what conditions, what accompanied it, and what the trend has been.

### Potentially urgent warning signs

The observation is consistent with something where delay carries real cost.

**Asks the reader to:** seek evaluation now rather than at the next convenient time.

Say what kind of care and what to mention. Then stop. Someone reading this may already be frightened,
and piling on consequences does not increase the chance they act — past a point it reduces it.

This tier escalates on a single observation. That is the deliberate exception to the trend-over-event
principle, and it exists because the cost of being wrong is asymmetric here in a way it is not
anywhere else ([Standard 12](../standards/12-red-flags.md) R1).

## Assigning a tier

Exactly one tier, chosen explicitly. Not two, not a range, not an implication left in the wording.

The inputs, in the order they usually matter:

1. **Individual baseline** ([Standard 7](../standards/07-individual-baseline.md)) — a resting heart
   rate of 48 is normal variation for the person who has lived at 48 and worth attention for the
   person who lived at 65 last month.
2. **Measurement quality** ([Standard 6](../standards/06-measurement-quality.md)) — a poor
   measurement earns *worth monitoring* far more often than it earns escalation.
3. **Trend versus event** ([Standard 2](../standards/02-trend-over-event.md)) — a single reading
   generally does not escalate past *worth monitoring*, except for red flags.
4. **Accompanying symptoms** ([Standard 4](../standards/04-symptom-context.md)) — symptoms usually
   carry more weight than the number they arrived with.
5. **Red flags** ([Standard 12](../standards/12-red-flags.md)) — these escalate on their own.

**Where these disagree, symptoms and red flags dominate.** A reassuring number does not lower the
tier that a worrying symptom set. A normal heart rate alongside chest pain does not make the chest
pain a lesser observation.

## Language

Escalation guidance must not create alarmism. This is not a softening of the tiers — it is what keeps
the top tier meaningful. A reader alarmed by ordinary variation learns to discount the system, and
the discounting is indiscriminate.

| | Do | Don't |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower tiers | State plainly. "This is within your ordinary range." | Borrow urgency. "Probably nothing, but you never know..." |
| Upper tiers | Say what to consider doing, and why. | Command panic, or list consequences for emphasis. |
| Any tier | Say what was observed and what it may reflect. | Name a condition. Escalation says *someone should look at this*, never *you have this*. |
| Uncertainty | Escalate on the seriousness, not the confidence. | Treat "we're not sure" as a reason to wait. |

The last row is the one most often got wrong. Uncertainty and urgency are independent axes, and
collapsing them produces false reassurance by a route that feels like intellectual honesty
([Standard 11](../standards/11-uncertainty.md) R6).

## Both directions of error are real

Over-escalation produces anxiety, unnecessary testing, and learned dismissal. Under-escalation delays
care. Neither is the safe default, and a project that treats one as the only real risk will
systematically commit the other.

The two prohibitions are symmetrical and both are in force:

- never [catastrophize ordinary measurements without supporting evidence](../PROHIBITIONS.md)
- never [provide false reassurance when serious warning signs are present](../PROHIBITIONS.md)
